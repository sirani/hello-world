Module 1 — Infrastructure · Q1

Training vs. Inference Infra

Original Rubric Question

You're talking to an IT Architect who says, "AI is just more compute." In their context, explain how different AI workloads (for example, real-time inference vs. large-scale training) drive different choices in compute, storage, and networking, and why those choices matter for performance and cost.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

AI isn't one workload — training and inference pull infrastructure in opposite directions. Training needs maximum throughput: dense GPU clusters, high-bandwidth memory, fast storage for data and checkpoints, and GPU-to-GPU fabric like InfiniBand, so expensive GPUs don't sit idle. Real-time inference needs predictable low latency: right-sized GPUs, fast local storage, efficient networking — nothing more than the request needs.

Trade-off: a dedicated training cluster speeds development but costs far more than a lean inference footprint; using one design for both wastes spend or creates bottlenecks.

Business outcome: aligning infrastructure to the workload hits performance targets while avoiding over-provisioning, runaway costs, and bottlenecks — exactly the split Dell's AI-optimized platforms are built for.

Example: Honeywell trains a predictive-maintenance model on plant sensor data using a dedicated GPU cluster with InfiniBand, cutting training from weeks to days, then deploys it on a smaller, latency-tuned inference setup near plant operations to flag equipment failures in real time.

Module 1 — Infrastructure · Q2

Shared App + AI Platform

Original Rubric Question

You're meeting a Head of Infrastructure who wants to run both traditional applications and AI workloads on the same platform. Explain how you would balance their infrastructure design across compute, storage, and networking to support both, and what trade-offs you would call out.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

Balance starts from your estate, not a generic blueprint. Compute is a mixed pool — CPU nodes for steady transactional apps, pooled GPU nodes for AI, with scheduling that flexes between them. Storage is tiered — reliable transactional storage for apps, high-throughput storage for AI, so neither starves the other. Networking is segmented, so bursty AI traffic doesn't congest everyday operations.

Trade-off: shared infrastructure lowers cost and improves utilization, but AI workloads can spike and compete with everyday apps for the same resources if not carefully isolated.

Business outcome: you run both cost-effectively on common infrastructure without one undermining the other's SLAs — built on Dell's AI-optimized platforms, straightforward to deploy into an existing estate.

Example: Honeywell runs its plant MES system alongside a new AI vision-inspection model on the same platform — pooled GPU nodes absorb the bursty inspection workload while a segmented network keeps the plant floor's control systems untouched.

Module 1 — Infrastructure · Q3

GenAI Pilot → Scale Platform

Original Rubric Question

A CTO wants to understand the infrastructure implications of "trying GenAI." Explain how you would help them distinguish between a small GenAI pilot and a scalable GenAI platform, and what changes in infrastructure requirements as they move from one to the other.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

Let's think of this as crawl, walk, run. Crawl — the pilot: a handful of users, narrow data, flexible SLAs, shared GPU capacity. Walk — departmental adoption: more users, firmer SLAs, dedicated GPU capacity, team isolation. Run — enterprise-wide: observability, governance, multi-tenancy, and resilience become non-negotiable because the business now depends on it.

Trade-off: a pilot is fast and cheap to stand up, but scaling it later usually means re-architecting rather than just adding resources.

Business outcome: planning the pilot with that path in mind avoids costly rework — the staged journey Dell's AI-optimized platforms and experts are built to support, controlling TCO at each stage.

Example: Honeywell's field-service team pilots a GenAI tool that summarizes technician service reports for one region on shared cloud GPUs. Six months later, rolling it out across every business unit requires dedicated clusters and multi-tenant governance the pilot never needed.

Module 2 — Orchestration · Q5

Scheduling & Auto-Rollbacks

Original Rubric Question

You're meeting with a Head of Engineering who already has a few AI services in production and wants to avoid incidents as usage grows. Explain how orchestration practices like scheduling, dependency management, observability, and automated rollbacks help keep those AI workloads stable over time.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

Picture a nightly model retrain. Scheduling ensures it gets the compute it needs without colliding with other jobs. Dependency management checks the data and prior model artifacts are ready before it runs. Observability — metrics, logs, traces — catches a quality drift before customers see it. If the new version is bad, automated rollback reverts to the last known-good state in minutes.

Trade-off: building this orchestration layer adds engineering overhead versus a simpler manual deploy process.

Business outcome: that overhead buys stable SLAs, fewer fire-drills, and confidence to keep experimenting as your AI footprint grows.

Example: Honeywell's predictive-maintenance service runs a nightly retrain on new sensor data. One night the retrain produces a model with a quality regression — observability flags it within minutes, and automated rollback reverts to the prior version before a single plant alert is affected.

Module 3 — Data Management · Q7

Warehouse vs. Lake vs. Lakehouse

Original Rubric Question

You're talking to a Head of Data whose mental model is still a single enterprise data warehouse. Explain how data lakes and data lakehouses differ from a traditional data warehouse, and why a lakehouse-style architecture is better suited to supporting AI workloads in terms of data types, performance, and agility.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

Let's break this into three layers, three jobs. A warehouse handles structured, relational data — great for reporting, rigid outside that lane. A lake stores anything — structured, unstructured, images, logs — cheaply at scale, but without warehouse-grade governance. A lakehouse unifies them: the lake's openness plus warehouse-grade governance and transactional reliability.

Trade-off: adopting a lakehouse means new tooling and governance practices versus staying in a familiar, already-governed warehouse.

Business outcome: shorter AI delivery cycles, better model quality from richer data, and flexibility to add new data types without re-platforming.

Example: Honeywell wants to combine maintenance logs, equipment sensor streams, and technician inspection photos to train one failure-prediction model. A warehouse-only approach forces pre-structuring everything first, costing months; a lakehouse lets the team bring it together as-is and iterate immediately.

Business outcome: fewer model failures caused by bad data, less firefighting, and AI products the organization can trust in production.

Module 4 — Data Engineering · Q10

Full Data Engineering Lifecycle

Original Rubric Question

End-to-end data engineering lifecycle—from ingest and storage through transformation, governance, feature engineering.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

The notebook pull is the last step, not the first. Before that: ingest pulls data from source systems; storage lands it durably; transformation cleans and reshapes it; governance applies access controls and lineage; feature engineering turns it into consistent model inputs; and serving delivers those features to production reliably and fast.

Trade-off: building out the full lifecycle with proper governance takes more coordination than letting people pull data ad hoc.

Business outcome: you remove the risk of models silently trained on inconsistent data — the real cause of costly production incidents.

Example: a Honeywell quality-defect model performs great in testing on a notebook pull, then fails in production because the live feature pipeline computes 'defect rate' differently than the notebook calculation did. Fixing that one feature-engineering stage would have caught it before deployment.

Module 4 — Data Engineering · Q11

Structured vs. Unstructured + Vectors

Original Rubric Question

A BI Lead has many dashboards on structured data and now wants to add GenAI on PDFs and documents. Explain how structured and unstructured data pipelines differ, including the role of embeddings and vector stores, and why those differences affect timelines, quality, and infrastructure choices.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

Your structured pipelines are ETL into a warehouse, queried with SQL — schema known upfront. Documents don't have that luxury: ingest, parse and extract text, chunk it, embed each chunk into a numerical representation of meaning, index it in a vector store, then retrieve by semantic similarity rather than exact match.

Trade-off: this needs new infrastructure — an embedding model, a vector database, GPU acceleration — extending timelines versus adding another table.

Business outcome: investing properly unlocks real GenAI capability while avoiding inaccurate outputs, so scope expectations should be set upfront.

Example: instead of adding a table, Honeywell's team extracts text from technical equipment manuals and compliance documents, embeds each section, and indexes it — so a technician asking 'what's the torque spec for this valve?' gets the exact relevant passage across thousands of manuals instantly.

Module 5 — AI Software · Q13

Traditional AI vs. ML vs. GenAI

Original Rubric Question

A Line-of-Business leader says, "We should just use GenAI for everything." In their context, explain the difference between traditional AI, machine learning, and generative AI, and give simple examples of when each is the better choice and why.

4/4 Interview-Ready Answer

Let's sort your actual decisions into three buckets. Rules-fit — approvals, compliance checks — a traditional rule-based system is faster, cheaper, fully auditable. Prediction-fit — forecasting demand, scoring risk — machine learning trained on historical data gives accurate outputs GenAI isn't built to produce reliably. Generation-fit — drafting, summarizing — generative AI excels because the task is language-heavy and open-ended.

Trade-off: the simpler, purpose-built approach gives up flexibility for open-ended tasks but gains accuracy, predictability, and lower cost for well-defined ones.

Business outcome: mapping each decision to the right AI type avoids wasted GenAI spend — exactly the mapping Dell can guide you through.

Example: Honeywell uses machine learning, not GenAI, to forecast spare-parts demand across its distribution centers, but turns to generative AI to draft the technician service summaries that go out after every maintenance visit.

Original Rubric Question

A CISO tells you, "We already have strong security; AI doesn't change much." Explain why AI introduces new risks around data volume, supply chain, model behavior, and regulation, and why a full-stack approach to securing AI is different from traditional security.

Traditional security focuses on perimeter, access, and code vulnerabilities. Trade-off: full-stack AI security — supply chain, operations, runtime — adds complexity and slows deployment versus relying on existing controls alone.

Business outcome: that layered approach protects you from risks traditional security was never designed to catch — which is why you need an AI-aware strategy, not incremental tweaks.

Example: Honeywell integrates a third-party pretrained model into its customer support chatbot. Without supply chain vetting, the model carries hidden biases from its fine-tuning data; without runtime monitoring, it starts giving inappropriate responses — a failure traditional perimeter security would never have caught.
